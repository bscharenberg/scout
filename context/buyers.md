# Who BTO sells to

BTO sells to two segments, in financial services, healthcare systems, industrial manufacturing, PE portfolio companies, and business services.

- **Enterprise** ($1B or more in revenue): C-suite buyers, multi-year transformations, heavy procurement.
- **Mid-market** ($150M to $1B in revenue): VP and director-level buyers, scoped engagements, lighter procurement, and relationships matter more than brand.

These are the people reps meet most. They are patterns, not real people.

## The new executive (CDO, CTO, COO, or similar in their first months)

What they care about: proving they were the right hire, not repeating their predecessor's mistakes, and showing the board or CEO a credible plan by a known date. They often inherit a stalled program and a team that is defensive about it.

How to approach them: be curious about why the last effort stalled before offering anything. Don't criticize the predecessor or the internal team. Some of those people may still be in the room or on the new executive's side. A short diagnostic that gives them an early, defensible win fits better than a big program.

## The enterprise CFO (economic buyer)

What they care about: knowing what they are buying, limiting risk, value inside the fiscal year, and the actual team rather than the pitch team.

How they buy: rarely the first contact, pulled in to approve scope and budget. Decides in one or two conversations. Asks a hard question once, and if the answer is weak they don't ask again.

What gets a yes: specific outcomes by specific dates, a fixed fee that caps exposure, and confidence BTO won't disappear after the final presentation. What gets a no: vague outcomes, a suspicion the senior person won't be around after week two, and feeling managed instead of advised.

## The mid-market VP of Strategy, COO, or SVP Operations (champion and often decision-maker)

What they care about: getting credit for bringing in a firm that helped, protecting their credibility if it goes wrong, practical output they can act on, and explaining the investment to their CEO in one sentence.

How they buy: often starts the conversation and makes the call, but may not have full budget authority and has to build the internal case. Wants to meet the team that will do the work. Personal trust matters more than the logo.

What gets a yes: trust in the person, a scope that feels the right size, and being able to picture presenting the work to their board. What gets a no: a proposal that reads like a template with a smaller logo, or not knowing what they get for the fee.

## The internal skeptic or blocker

Who: the head of internal strategy, a loyalist to an incumbent firm, or a procurement lead. Can slow or kill a deal without being the economic buyer.

Common moves: asking for a formal RFP, pointing to a long relationship with a larger firm, saying the internal team can do it, or saying the timing is wrong.

How to handle: don't fight the blocker directly. Help the champion bring them in rather than go around them. For RFP pressure, take part while building the economic buyer relationship in parallel. For loyalty to an incumbent, ask about what hasn't worked without attacking the other firm. For "our team can do it", position BTO as outside perspective and air cover, not replacement headcount. For timing, find out whether it's budget or alignment, then work on the real one.
