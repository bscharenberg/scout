# Common objections and how BTO answers them

These are starting points, not scripts. A rep should always acknowledge the objection first and then answer it in their own words.

**"You're too expensive compared to the Big 4."**
A lower number at proposal stage is not always a lower number at invoice stage. The difference is usually staffing: many large-firm proposals price a pyramid of junior people with a senior partner who appears at milestones, while BTO's price reflects the senior team that does the work. Don't assert this about a specific competitor's proposal. Ask questions that let the buyer see it: who is actually doing the work, how many partner hours are in each proposal, and what each one assumes about change orders and a second phase. If the buyer still needs a lower number, reduce the scope or start with a diagnostic sprint. Don't discount the fee.

**"We've used consultants before and the work sat on a shelf."**
That's the most common outcome in the industry, and it happens for a predictable reason: the work was done for the client, not with them. BTO builds the answer with the people who have to carry it out.

**"We need deeper industry expertise."**
Ask what specifically they're trying to solve. Industry knowledge matters most for benchmarks and market knowledge. For strategy and operating model work, the limit is almost never industry knowledge. It's asking the right questions and getting a leadership team that disagrees to agree.

**"The timing isn't right. Maybe next quarter."**
Timing usually means one of two things: the budget isn't secured, or the leadership team isn't aligned. Ask which one. The next step is different for each.

**"We want case studies from our exact industry."**
BTO can share relevant reference work through approved references. It's also fair to push back gently: the most useful references faced the same type of problem, not always the same industry. The sales assistant never makes up a reference or a result.

**"We're going to run an RFP."**
BTO will take part, and the rep should ask one question: is this a real competitive process, or is procurement requiring a process around a decision that's mostly made? If it's real, compete. If it's not, help make it efficient for everyone.

**"Your firm is too small for something this size."**
Size matters for staffing risk, and that's a fair concern. The real question is whether they want a large team or the right team. If they want forty consultants on site, BTO isn't the fit. If they want the six people who will solve the problem, show them exactly who those people are.

**"We need someone who can also do the implementation."**
That's the conflict of interest worth naming. An advisor with implementation revenue at stake has a reason to recommend the solution that creates the most work. BTO helps the client make the decision and then choose the best implementer. Independence is the value.
